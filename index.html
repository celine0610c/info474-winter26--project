For the 15% of Seattle residents living with a disability, a crumbling curb ramp is not an inconvenience — it is a barrier. When a ramp is rated "poor" or "very poor," it can render entire blocks impassable for wheelchair users, people with visual impairments, and parents with strollers.

The City of Seattle's Department of Transportation tracks every curb ramp in the city — its condition, installation date, and location. What that data reveals is a patchwork of maintenance: some neighborhoods dense with new, compliant ramps; others relying on infrastructure decades past its useful life.

This investigation examines that data across six dimensions — from the overall condition breakdown, to how maintenance has shifted over time, to which districts bear the heaviest burden of deteriorating infrastructure.

01.

The Condition Breakdown

All 46,126 curb ramps by condition rating. Hover a bar to see counts and percentages.

While a large share of ramps are rated "good," the combined total in fair, poor, and very poor condition represents a significant maintenance backlog — infrastructure that people with disabilities navigate every single day.

What these aggregate numbers obscure is where those failing ramps are concentrated. The sections below reveal a pattern tied closely to neighborhood wealth.

02.

A City Built in Waves

Curb ramp installations per year — showing how ADA legislation and city budget cycles shaped the network over time.

Installation history is not a steady upward curve. It's punctuated by spikes tied to ADA mandates, infrastructure bonds, and neighborhood improvement projects. Ramps from the 1980s and 90s are now aging into "poor" ratings at an accelerating rate.

03.

A Geography of Neglect

Percentage of ramps in good condition by Seattle neighborhood. Green areas are more accessible, red areas have the most deteriorating infrastructure. Hover a neighborhood to see the breakdown. Click to zoom in.

When you map ramp conditions across Seattle's neighborhoods, a clear geographic pattern emerges. It is not random. Neighborhoods in South Seattle consistently have lower percentages of good-condition ramps compared to wealthier areas further north. For residents who rely on curb ramps daily, this gap is not just a statistic — it shapes whether they can independently navigate their own block.

Note: neighborhoods shown in gray had too few ramps with recorded data to calculate a reliable score.

05.

Age vs. Condition: The Neighborhoods Falling Behind

Each dot is a neighborhood. X axis is average ramp age, Y axis is % of ramps in poor or fair condition. Dot size reflects total number of ramps. Hover a dot to identify the neighborhood.

The trend line tells a clear story — older ramps are more likely to be in poor condition. But the outliers are just as revealing. Some neighborhoods with relatively new ramps still have high failure rates, suggesting maintenance gaps that go beyond age alone. Others have old infrastructure that has been kept in good shape — pointing to where investment has actually happened.

Methodology & Data Notes

All data sourced from the Seattle Open Data Portal's Curb Ramps dataset, maintained by SDOT. The dataset contains 46,126 records as of the time of analysis.

Condition ratings are assigned by SDOT field inspectors. Blank condition fields are categorized as "Unknown." Installation dates parsed from INSTALL_DATE; malformed records excluded from timeline analysis. District data uses PRIMARYDISTRICTCD.
